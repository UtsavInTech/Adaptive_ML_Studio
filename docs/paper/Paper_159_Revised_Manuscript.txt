Abstract.With the emergence of continuous and constantly chang-
ing data streams, the traditional machine learning pipeline, which is
customarily built around static datasets and periodic retraining, shows
clear limitations. Adaptive preprocessing, online feature selection, con-
cept drift detection, incremental learning, and adaptive model updating
are examples of adaptive machine learning techniques that help mod-
els follow varying data distributions. Existing systems tend to focus on
predictive performance without considering latency or computational
efficiency, which is problematic for real-time and resource-constrained
environments. In this paper, we review key studies from the adaptive
learning field, with emphasis on latency, adaptation mechanisms, and
technologies. The included studies are compared in terms of the adapta-
tions they use and their performance in prediction, processing efficiency,
and deployment characteristics. We also conduct a comparative analysis
to identify progress and gaps between predictive accuracy and latency
for edge devices with limited memory and computational resources. The
review highlights the need for an integrated, latency-sensitive adaptive
machine learning framework that can switch its learning and process-
ing approach dynamically as data and resource usage change. A multi-
attributecomparison,ananalysisoftotaladaptationcost,andapipeline-
stage analysis are provided. The proposed framework is a conceptual di-
rection for future research and has not been experimentally validated;
the reported results are specific to the individual studies and are not
normalised comparisons.

streaming data can show distribution drift and fundamental changes in data

state of the art together with its limitations and research gaps, and it motivates
the development of an accuracy–latency-aware adaptive ML framework for data
streams that evolve over time.
2 Background and Literature Review
Adaptive machine learning refers to the ability of a learning system to change its
behaviour when the data change or when more data become available over time.
Unlike conventional models trained on a fixed data set, adaptive models can
be updated continuously and adjusted to changing data distributions. Several
studies have addressed different aspects of adaptation: preprocessing, feature
selection, model learning, and adaptation pipelines [3,6,18,20].
Concept drift occurs when the underlying distribution or relationship in a
data stream changes over time, which can degrade the predictions of a model
thatpreviouslyworkedwell.Theadaptivewindowingapproach,suchasADWIN,
detects statistically significant change and adjusts the window of historical data
used for learning [5].
Incremental learning lets a model improve progressively as new observations
arrive, without retraining on the whole data set. Adaptive online incremental
learning has been studied for evolving data streams [18], and more recent con-
tinual and generalised incremental learning methods aim to retain learned knowl-
edge in the presence of concept drift [1,2]. These approaches can reduce the need
for repeated full-scale training, but they may increase memory and computation
requirements, depending on how often the model is updated.
TinyML aims to deploy machine learning on very resource-constrained
devices with limited memory, processing power, and energy supply. For data

The comparison in Table 1 shows which dimensions are commonly and rarely
evaluated. Every study is characterised by its adaptation mechanism and by a
qualitative limitation, but predictive metrics appear quantitatively for only a
small subset of the summarised studies ([7,15,19,20]), and latency or runtime
for fewer still ([11,15]). Memory is mostly described qualitatively, and energy
consumption is not reported as a separate quantity for any entry in the material
summarised here.
Model-adaptation studies ([1,2,7,18,20]) emphasise how a learner retains
or recovers accuracy under drift, and their limitations concern memory, com-
putation, or abrupt drift. Edge-oriented studies ([11–15]) emphasise resources
and deployment: [11] reports latency per deployment configuration, [15] reports
per-instance runtime together with prequential accuracy, and [12–14] describe
resource-aware or on-device adaptation. Preprocessing, feature-selection, and or-
chestrationstudies([3,4,6,8,16,17,19])addresstheconfigurationorautomation
of pipeline stages rather than edge resource use.
Accuracy, latency, memory, and adaptation cost are therefore evaluated sep-
arately: [20] reports accuracy, [11] reports deployment-level latency, and [15]
reports accuracy and runtime for a decision-tree family but at the model stage
only. In the material summarised here, no entry reports the cost of preprocessing,
feature selection, drift detection, and model update as parts of one end-to-end
measure. This is why a unified evaluation, in which accuracy, latency, resource
use, and adaptation cost are measured under the same conditions, remains nec-
essary.

Conceptdriftdetection[5,7,13,14].Adaptivewindowing[5],probabilis-

overhead, and the resulting accuracy, nor how the detection signal coordinates
other stages. Drift detection is thus best treated as the trigger that supplies a
drift status to a controller, with its own overhead counted.
Incremental and ensemble learning [1,2,18,20].These studies retain
or recover accuracy without full retraining [1,2,18,20]. They measure predictive
performance, but update time, memory growth, and energy for repeated updates
are not reported separately in the summarised material. This implies that model-
update time and model size must be reported, and that a lightweight adaptation
mode is needed when resources are scarce.
Pipeline orchestration, AutoML, and streaming frameworks
[6,8–10].These works automate data selection and retraining [6], model
adaptation [8], distributed stream mining [9], and lightweight stream learning
[10]. They measure automation and scalability rather than execution under
constrained resources. Orchestration decisions should therefore be linked to the
resource state of the target device.
Edge and resource-aware approaches [11–15].These studies address
edge-cloud deployment [11], resource-aware analytics [12], edge drift detection
[13], TinyML adaptation [14], and edge decision trees [15]. They measure deploy-
ment latency [11] and runtime and memory trade-offs [15], but not a complete
adaptive pipeline, including preprocessing, feature selection, and model update,
under changing resources. This motivates adaptation that responds jointly to
drift and to resource state.
Taken together, adaptive preprocessing reduces the cost associated with
changing input characteristics, while online feature selection reduces unneces-
sary feature computation. Drift detection provides the trigger for adaptation,
and incremental learning updates the model. However, these components are
frequently evaluated separately rather than as one end-to-end adaptive pipeline,
and the reviewed evidence does not show how their costs combine.

Latency and efficiency values in the reviewed studies were measured under het-

ueswouldrequireassumptionsthatthesourcestudiesdonotsupport;thisreview
therefore does not normalise them. Instead, it specifies the items that a future
common evaluation protocol should report.Hardware.Processor architecture,
CPU frequency or class, RAM, accelerator (GPU or NPU) where applicable,
and edge device category.Dataset.Dataset type, number of features, stream
length, and drift characteristics (drift type and frequency).Model.Model archi-

feature-selection latency, drift-detection latency, inference latency, model-update
latency, retraining latency, and total end-to-end pipeline latency, reported sep-

a single average.Resource.CPU utilisation, memory usage, energy consump-
tion, storage, and communication overhead. Until such a protocol is adopted,
the values in Table 1 should be read as study-specific rather than as normalised
head-to-head benchmarks.
Inference latency alone does not describe the cost of an adaptive system.
This review distinguishes three quantities.Inference latencyis the time to
produce a prediction with the current model.Adaptation latencyis the time
consumed by the operations that keep the pipeline current: preprocessing up-
dates, online feature-selection updates, drift detection, and incremental model
updates(model-updatetime).Retraininglatencyisthetimeneededtorebuild
or substantially revise the model, in full or on a buffer of recent data (retraining
overhead).
The total adaptation cost is defined here conceptually as the sum of these
components, each of which has a time, memory, and energy footprint:
Ctotal =C pre +C fs +C drift +C inf +C upd +C ret +C comm (1)
A system with low inference latency can still have a high system-level cost if it
triggers expensive updates or retraining frequently, because the cost per update
accumulates with the update frequency, which depends on drift frequency and
detector sensitivity. On memory-limited devices, buffers and ensembles increase
memory use [1,20], and on energy-limited devices repeated updates can shorten
operating time. Resource-aware and TinyML studies [12,14,15] consider mem-
ory, runtime, or computational limits, but update and retraining phases are not
reported separately as latency or energy quantities in the summarised material.
This omission is carried into the research gaps of Section 5 and into the metrics
of the proposed direction in Section 6.

5 Research Gaps
Although the reviewed studies demonstrate continued development in adaptive
machine learning for evolving data streams, several research gaps remain.
One of the drawbacks is that there is not much integration between the
pipeline stages. Most methods focus on a specific aspect, whereas only few stud-
ies present a single mechanism that can address the different aspects of pre-
processing, feature selection, drift detection, model adaptation and continuous
monitoring [3,6,18].
Another gap that is important is the consideration of predictive accuracy
and latency together. Latency, runtime, and computational efficiency are not
evaluated using consistent metrics across the reviewed studies [6,11,12,20].
The comparative analysis of Section 4 exposes further gaps concerning mea-
surement and cost. Latency and resource use are measured inconsistently across
studies, and on heterogeneous hardware and deployment settings, so reported
values cannot be normalised (Section 4.3). Model-update and retraining over-
head are seldom reported as separate latency, memory, or energy quantities, and

6 Proposed Research Direction
The identified research gaps motivate a conceptual, integrated adaptive machine
learning framework as a research direction for evolving data streams with a focus
on predictive accuracy, latency and resource efficiency.
The proposed research direction is to investigate an accuracy–latency-aware
adaptive machine learning framework for edge-based streaming environments.
The framework will include adaptive preprocessing, concept drift detection, in-
cremental learning and online feature selection to facilitate the continuous adap-
tation of models. Furthermore, system-level monitoring will be included to track

6.1 Conceptual Architecture and Workflow
Fig. 2 shows the intended architecture. Data pass through stream ingestion,
adaptive preprocessing, online feature selection, concept drift detection, and
incremental learning or model adaptation before a prediction is produced. A
system and resource monitor observes CPU utilisation, memory usage, latency,
and energy consumption during operation. An adaptation controller (decision
engine) receives the drift statusDand the predictive accuracyAfrom the data
path, and the resource status (C,M,L,E) from the monitor. It returns control
actions: a change of preprocessing configuration, a change of the active feature
set, model selection or switching to a lightweight model, an incremental update,
or retraining.
Data-driven adaptation responds to changes in the stream: the drift statusD
and the observed accuracyAindicate whether the current pipeline configuration
still matches the data. Resource-driven adaptation responds to system state: la-
tencyL, computational costC, memory utilisationM, and energy consumption

Eindicate whether the pipeline can sustain its configuration on the target de-

separately (Section 5).
6.2 Adaptation Policy and Decision Rules
LetAdenote predictive accuracy (or F1-score) over a sliding window,Lla-
tency,Ccomputational cost (corresponding toC total in Section 4.3),Mmemory
utilisation,Eenergy consumption, andDthe drift status. Application-defined
thresholdsA min,L max,C max,M max, andE max express the requirements of a
deployment; resources are regarded as sufficient whenC≤C max,M≤M max,
andE≤E max. The controller would evaluate the following conceptual rules on
each monitoring window.
R1. IFconcept drift is detectedANDresource availability is sufficient
THENperform incremental model adaptation.
R2. IFconcept drift is detectedANDmemory or CPU constraints are
severeTHENuse a lightweight adaptation strategy.
R3. IFL>L max THENreduce preprocessing complexity, reduce the
feature set, or switch to a lower-complexity model.
R4.IFA<A min ANDresources are availableTHENinitiate stronger
model adaptation or retraining.
R5.IFM>M max THENreduce model or buffer complexity, or switch
to a resource-efficient configuration.
When rules conflict, for example when accuracy has fallen belowA min while
memory exceedsM max, resource limits are treated as hard constraints, and
stronger adaptation is deferred or replaced by a lightweight alternative until
resources allow. These rules are conceptual mechanisms. Their thresholds, order-
ing, and effect on accuracy and latency have not been validated in this review
and are subject to the planned experiments.
6.3 Multi-objective Formulation
The controller is intended to select a pipeline configurationθ(preprocessing
setting, active feature subset, model, and update strategy) that minimises the
objective
J=α(1−A) +βL+γC+δM+εE(2)
subject toL≤L max,M≤M max, andE≤E max. HereA∈[0, 1] is predictive
accuracy over a sliding window;L,C,M, andEare normalised by application-
specific reference values so that the terms are commensurate; andα,β,γ,δ,
andεare non-negative, application-dependent weights. This is a proposed opti-
misation formulation for future experimental validation; it is not a result of the
current review, and the choice of weights, the normalisation, and the search over
configurations remain open design questions.

6.4 Planned Evaluation
In future work, the proposed framework will be evaluated on evolving data-
stream datasets and compared with existing adaptive learning approaches under
the common reporting protocol of Section 4.3. The planned metrics are accu-
racy, F1-score, prediction latency, end-to-end pipeline latency, drift-detection
latency, model-update time, retraining time, CPU utilisation, memory usage,
energy consumption, computational overhead, and, where relevant, communica-
tion overhead.
7 Conclusion
This review examined adaptive machine learning approaches for evolving data
streams, covering adaptive preprocessing, online feature selection, concept drift
detection, incremental learning, streaming frameworks, pipeline orchestration,
and edge-based learning. The comparative analysis reveals that existing meth-
ods are able to handle the changing data distribution, but the accuracy, latency
and resource utilization are not comprehensively measured simultaneously. The
unified comparison and the component-wise synthesis show that the reviewed
studies differ in datasets, hardware, and measurement protocols, so their nu-
merical values are study-specific rather than normalised head-to-head results.
Model-update and retraining overhead and stage-level attribution of end-to-end
cost are rarely reported.
The review highlights that limited integration is seen throughout adaptive
pipeline stages and no common evaluation settings for predictive and system-
level performance. These gaps motivate a conceptual adaptive ML framework
that considers data drift and resource constraints jointly through an adaptation
controller, decision rules, and a multi-objective formulation; the framework has
not been experimentally validated.
